Vast, never-ending fields, guarded by tall volcanoes from all directions. The air is full of animal scent and the grave silence is periodically cut with the sounds of cattle bells. 

No, I am not in the Wild West, I am on a plateau of Massif Central in France. Parc naturel régional de l'Aubrac. The little cowboy-me is jumping, but something strange is looming from those tall and lonely gentians and aggresive eyes of Aubrac bulls. 

Only later I will realise, I'm in a fever dream.

## Location and its values
Lozère is the least-densely populated department in France. I stayed two weeks in Nasbinals, a 500-people village in the heart of Aubrac. The closest supermaket, in knife-famous Laguiole was 30 mins by car. The infinete, rolling plains and isolation from everything will plant seeds of doubt into you – not to get back to the city, but to rethink your life and values.

There is something special about losing your cellular connection. 

## Dreams and what is lost 
My mind often wanders to the beatiful landscape, to the places, smells, windblows or sunsets. It wants to go back. But of course, you can go, Dominik. You already did. But you know it isn't possible.

Because fever dreams are not about the place. They're about the moments and the absurdity of being [unreachable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentz_transformation). The inability to reach them, fueling nostalgia are 


Some places you visit. Others you carry home without realising, folded into the lining of something, and only later — months later, reaching for something else entirely — do you find them there.

Aubrac and Nasbinals are the second kind. If only I knew how and whether I want to wake up from this fever dream.

---

The plateau sits above most things: above the valleys, above the treeline in places, above the noise that most of Europe runs on. The Aubrac in June is green in a way that feels almost deliberate, volcanic hills holding the horizon still, the air arriving cold from somewhere unaccounted for. You expect summer and get something older — a climate with its own logic, indifferent to the calendar.

I spent two weeks there and learned very little about the place. I learned more about myself than I wanted to.

There was a horse. There was the slow, humbling work of trying to trust something that does not speak your language. There were mornings that started before the light had settled and evenings that ended in the kind of laughter that doesn't need a reason. There were dinners. There were rides out into the open plateau, where the sky is very large and the self becomes proportionally smaller in a way that is, eventually, a relief.

I had an identity crisis in a paddock in the south of France, which sounds absurd until it happens to you, and then it sounds entirely inevitable.

---

The last morning, the water was cold. The horse didn't mind. I didn't mind either, and that was how I knew something had quietly shifted.

Then I left, because you always leave.

---

The road south to north — or north to wherever you're pointed — has its own rhythm. A high mountain pass climbed alone in the early light, the kind of suffering that purifies rather than breaks. A city where a bird reminded me that grandeur is always one indignity away from comedy. Canals at dusk. Heat that pressed down like a hand. Three books read slowly. A lens I didn't need and don't regret.

The trip was my first alone. I discovered that solitude, properly entered, is not absence. It is a different kind of company — your own, which turns out to be interesting when you finally stop apologising for it.

---

What happened to Nasbinals, in the months after, is harder to explain.

Memory is not a neutral container. It edits, it saturates, it makes the light better than it was. By the time winter came, the plateau had undergone some transformation I didn't choose — it had become luminous in my head, distant and lit strangely, the way a photograph looks when it's been left somewhere warm. By spring it felt like something I had dreamed rather than done.

*A fever dream*, I wrote in my journal. *Everything from it feels so distant now but so beautiful.*

I don't know what to do with a place that means that much. I don't know if it means the place was extraordinary or if it means I was, in that particular moment, simply open — open in the way you only get when the ordinary structures of your life are temporarily suspended and something else can come in.

Maybe it was both. Maybe the distinction doesn't hold.

---

The plateau is still there. The horse is still there. The cold stream and the wide sky and the dinners that ended late — all still there, continuing without me, indifferent to the mythology I've built around them.

I keep returning anyway. Not by road. In memory, which is a reliable enough route when the other ones are closed. I carry the place in the lining of something. I find it when I'm not looking.

I don't think I'll stop.
