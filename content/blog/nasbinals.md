📍 Aubrac plateau · Lozère, France — June–July 2025

My first solo road trip didn't start solo. It started in Zurich, over a coffee and a ride with a friend, and only became solitary somewhere on the A75 heading south into the Massif Central with a week-old playlist and the particular kind of quiet that fills a car when you're finally moving toward something.

Nasbinals is small. The kind of small that barely registers on a regional map — a cluster of stone houses on the Aubrac plateau in Lozère, surrounded by open pasture and volcanic hills and air that feels ten degrees cooler than it should in late June. The population thickens once a year when trail runners arrive for a race weekend, filling the café and spilling across the square, and then it empties again and the cows reclaim the roads.

I spent two weeks there, working mornings from a kitchen table, eating dinner late with the family, and spending the hours in between trying to learn to ride a horse.

## Corrida

The horse's name was Corrida. He was patient with me in a way that felt less like tolerance and more like mild amusement. Horse riding, I discovered, is one of those things that looks graceful from the outside and is entirely humbling from within it. I failed more rides than I completed. The saddle, the posture, the trust required — all of it came slowly, or not at all. There were days when I couldn't get out of the field, days when I stood in the paddock feeling genuinely uncertain whether this was the sort of thing I would ever be able to do, and days when that feeling bled into something larger and less nameable — an identity crisis conducted in a paddock in rural France, which is both absurd and completely serious.

Then a lesson finally worked. And then another. The progress was thin but real.

The last morning, we rode bareback into a stream. The water was cold, Corrida indifferent to the whole arrangement, and I was laughing in the way you laugh when something is briefly and completely perfect.

## The plateau

The Aubrac plateau in summer is wide and green and unpredictable. Sun one hour, cold wind the next. The trail rides went out onto it — long, slow walks through pasture with volcanic rock underfoot and views that felt borrowed from somewhere further north. I spent one Sunday on a bike ride that nearly ended badly when an insect flew into my mouth at 70 km/h on a descent. I survived it with nothing but a story. The hills cost me something by the afternoon.

One evening we drove to a wolf park. I had already spent a week in Slovak wilderness reading wolf tracks in snow a few months prior, so seeing wolves behind a fence carried a complicated charge. They were magnificent and clearly unhappy about the fence, which felt about right.

Another evening we went to a waterfall. We took photographs. Some of them came out well.

## L'Annexe

The restaurant was nothing special — food, wine, good enough — but the dinners at L'Annexe were consistent punctuation marks on the days. The kind of meal that functions less as eating and more as decompression. I had one there on my last night, and it closed the trip in the way only unremarkable dinners can: without ceremony, which is its own kind of grace.

## After Nasbinals

I drove to Briançon the next morning with something heavy in the car that wasn't luggage.

The objective was Col du Granon — a Tour de France climb above the city, steep and exposed and famous. I'd wanted to ride it for years. The morning was clear. I clipped in and went. It hurt in the way that only the right kind of suffering does, and when I reached the top I sat for a while in the wind and didn't need to think about anything. A pigeon shat on me in Torino that evening. The universe maintaining balance.

Venice came a day later — arrived in brutal heat, bought a camera lens in Padova that I didn't strictly need, walked through the crowds and canals and felt genuinely glad to be there, while also, unavoidably, missing the plateau. That is the strange mathematics of a trip like this: the next place is always partly about the last one.

Croatia was rest. Reading, swimming, a few bike rides. I finished three books. I watched the Tour de France. I let the journey settle.

## What it became

By the time I got home, I understood something about solo travel that I'd only suspected before: I like my own company more than I'd given myself credit for. I liked the mornings with no one to negotiate with, the routes chosen on a whim, the freedom that is, underneath its romance, simply the absence of friction. I want more of it.

The Nasbinals memory did something odd in the months that followed. It mythologised itself. Not immediately — first it was just a trip, then a good trip, then something that kept surfacing unprompted. By spring of the following year it had become what I can only describe as a fever dream: distant and lit strangely, like a photograph left in the sun. I kept returning to it not for any single moment but for the whole texture of it — the plateau in the early morning, the horse, the sound of French spoken quickly across a kitchen table, the particular cold of that stream.

Some trips you process and file away. Others do something different. They quietly become part of how you see yourself — a reference point you didn't choose, a place you keep going back to in your head long after you've left.

I left something in Nasbinals. I'm still not entirely sure what. But I know I'll keep going back, at least for now, the only way that's available to me: in memory, which turns out to be a reliable enough road.
